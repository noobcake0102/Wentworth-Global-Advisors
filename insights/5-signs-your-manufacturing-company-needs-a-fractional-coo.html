5 Signs Your Manufacturing Company Needs a Fractional COO

Fractional COO By Nick Bobay January 20, 2025 9 min read

Most manufacturing CEOs know something is wrong before they can name it. Deliveries slip. The calendar fills with problems that should never reach the CEO's desk. Revenue grows and somehow margins shrink. If any of that sounds familiar, this article is a diagnostic. Here are the five signs your manufacturing company needs a fractional COO — and what to do about each one.

The Decision That Most Manufacturing CEOs Wait Too Long to Make

The question of when to hire a fractional COO is not a philosophical one. It has concrete, observable answers — patterns that show up on the production floor, in the P&L, in the board meeting, and on the CEO's calendar. The difficulty is not identifying these patterns in retrospect. It is recognizing them in the moment, before the cost of inaction compounds.

A fractional COO is a senior operating executive who holds full operational authority on a part-time schedule — typically two to four days per week. They are not a consultant who presents recommendations. They manage people, own the production process, and are accountable to hard numbers. The fractional model is most powerful at the moment a manufacturing company needs that level of leadership but cannot justify — or cannot wait for — a full-time COO hire.

Below are the five situations where the need is clearest, along with the indicators that distinguish a real problem from a temporary rough patch.

The 5 Signs

Sign 1: The CEO or Founder Is Spending More Than 40% of Their Time on Operational Firefighting

This is the most common entry point for a fractional COO engagement, and the most damaging pattern when left unaddressed. A manufacturing CEO who is spending nearly half their working hours managing production escalations, supplier crises, quality escapes, and scheduling conflicts is not running a business — they are running an operation. Those are different jobs, and conflating them is expensive.

The business cost is straightforward: every hour the CEO spends on the floor is an hour not spent on customer relationships, strategic planning, talent development, and the work that only the CEO can do. The operational cost is subtler but equally real: when the CEO is the de facto COO, the operations leadership team stops developing. They learn to escalate rather than solve. They wait for direction instead of building judgment. The CEO's presence in the operational role — even a well-intentioned presence — actively inhibits the organizational capability the company needs to scale.

The 40% threshold is not arbitrary. Research on executive time allocation consistently shows that CEOs who spend more than 40% of their time on internal operational management significantly underperform on revenue growth and strategic execution compared to those who maintain the external and strategic focus the role demands. For manufacturing companies, that threshold is if anything conservative — the operational pull is stronger, and the feedback loops that draw CEOs into firefighting are more immediate and viscerally rewarding than strategic work.

Ask yourself honestly: what would your calendar look like this week if every operational problem resolved itself? If the answer is that your week would look fundamentally different, a fractional COO is not a luxury — it is the structural fix that unlocks everything else you are trying to build.

  • You are personally approving or expediting production schedules more than twice per week
  • Customer escalations reliably land on your desk before they are resolved at the operations level
  • Your direct reports in operations ask for guidance on decisions they should own independently
  • Strategic initiatives — a new product line, a key account pursuit, a capital raise — are consistently delayed because of operational demands on your time

Sign 2: On-Time Delivery Is Declining and No One Owns It

On-time delivery is the single most important operational metric in manufacturing. It is the number that customers see, that purchasing managers track, and that supplier scorecards are built around. A sustained OTD decline is not a scheduling problem or a supply chain problem or a quality problem. It is a leadership problem. Someone with the authority and the accountability to own that number is not present in the organization.

The "no one owns it" dynamic is more common than it sounds. In many manufacturing companies below $50M in revenue, OTD performance is diffuse — it is nominally the responsibility of production scheduling, with dependencies on supply chain, quality, and engineering, and with ultimate accountability that technically rolls up to the CEO or a general manager who has fifteen other things on their plate. When a metric has that many owners, it has no owner. The result is predictable: OTD declines gradually, everyone points to contributing factors, no one takes a clean accountability position, and customers begin to notice before the internal team acknowledges the trend.

A fractional COO creates a single locus of operational accountability. OTD belongs to them. They own the number in the weekly leadership review, in the board report, and in the conversation with the customer. That clarity of ownership changes how the organization behaves — because now there is a consequence for underperformance that is not abstract.

The diagnostic question is not just "is OTD declining?" It is: "Who would I call at 7 AM if OTD dropped five points this week and I needed someone to own the recovery plan by end of day?" If the answer is uncertain — or if the answer is "me" — the organizational gap is real.

  • On-time delivery has declined more than five percentage points over any rolling three-month period
  • Customer complaints about delivery timing are increasing quarter over quarter
  • Root cause analysis for missed deliveries routinely surfaces "scheduling" or "supply chain" without a clear corrective action owner
  • Your operations leadership team disagrees about what the current OTD number actually is

Sign 3: The Company Just Crossed $15M–$20M in Revenue and Operational Complexity Outpaced the Team

There is a well-documented inflection point in manufacturing company growth that occurs somewhere between $15M and $25M in revenue. Below that threshold, a skilled founder-operator and a strong production manager can manage most operational complexity through proximity, informal communication, and force of will. Above it, that approach breaks down — not because the people become less capable, but because the system has grown beyond what any informal coordination mechanism can manage.

The symptoms are recognizable. Scheduling becomes a negotiation rather than a system. Supply chain decisions are made reactively rather than proactively. Quality escapes increase as throughput pressure mounts. New hires do not ramp as quickly because there is no structured onboarding or standard work for them to follow. Gross margins compress despite growing revenue, because operational inefficiency is scaling faster than the revenue that was supposed to absorb it.

This is the moment that requires operational infrastructure — scheduling systems, supplier scorecards, quality management cadences, production performance dashboards, and a leadership structure with clear accountabilities — and it requires someone with the experience to build that infrastructure while simultaneously keeping the operation running. That is a specific and demanding combination of skills. It is exactly what an experienced fractional COO provides.

The alternative — waiting until the team figures it out organically, or promoting an internally developed production manager into an operational leadership role before they are ready — is not a neutral choice. Every month of operational drift at this stage translates directly into margin erosion, customer attrition risk, and organizational capability gaps that become harder to close as the company continues to grow.

  • Revenue has grown 20% or more in the last 12 months but gross margins have not improved — or have declined
  • The company has added headcount but output per employee has not scaled proportionally
  • Scheduling, quality, and supply chain decisions are made in ad hoc conversations rather than through defined processes
  • The founding team is running the operation the same way it did at half the current revenue

Sign 4: A Post-Merger Integration Is Stalling or Underperforming

Post-merger integration is the highest-stakes operational challenge in manufacturing. The thesis that justified the acquisition — cost synergies, capacity consolidation, market expansion, technology acquisition — does not realize itself. It has to be executed. And execution requires sustained operational leadership that most acquired manufacturing companies, by definition, do not have sitting on the bench.

The failure modes are consistent. Two companies with different ERP systems, different quality standards, different scheduling philosophies, and different organizational cultures are combined under a timeline that made sense in the deal model but proves optimistic in contact with reality. Key talent from the acquired company exits during the uncertainty of the transition. The acquiring company's operational leadership is stretched across the integration and their base business simultaneously. Customers begin to experience service disruptions. The synergies that justified the purchase price recede.

A fractional COO who has managed post-merger integrations before — who has a methodology, who knows the sequence of decisions that need to be made and in what order, and who has the operating authority to make them — compresses the integration timeline significantly. They bring pattern recognition to a situation where the internal team is often experiencing everything for the first time. They can also serve as a neutral bridge between the two organizations, without the political baggage that comes with being perceived as "from the acquirer" or "from the acquired company."

For private equity sponsors managing platform acquisitions with multiple add-ons, a fractional COO who spans multiple portfolio companies — building the operational playbook once and deploying it repeatedly — delivers compounding value that a single-company full-time hire cannot replicate.

  • The integration is more than 90 days old and operational synergies have not yet been realized
  • Customer service levels at the acquired company have declined since close
  • Key technical or operational personnel from the acquired company have resigned or are actively disengaged
  • The integration project plan exists on paper but lacks a single executive accountable for day-to-day execution

Sign 5: The Board or PE Sponsor Is Losing Confidence in Operational Execution

When the board or a private equity sponsor begins expressing concern about operational performance, the window for a proactive response is shorter than most CEOs appreciate. Board and sponsor confidence is not rebuilt through explanation — it is rebuilt through demonstrated operational improvement, and that improvement has to be visible within a quarter or two. The political reality is unforgiving: a CEO who is seen as unable to solve the operational problem is at risk, regardless of their strength in other dimensions of the role.

Bringing in a fractional COO is one of the most credible signals a CEO can send to the board in this situation. It demonstrates self-awareness about the operational gap. It shows that the CEO is prioritizing the business outcome over protecting organizational territory. And it gives the board something concrete to evaluate — a senior operator with specific credentials and a defined scope of work, accountable to measurable milestones.

In PE-backed manufacturing companies specifically, the fractional COO often becomes an important interface between the portfolio company's operational leadership and the sponsor's operating partners. That interface — when managed well — improves information flow, builds sponsor confidence, and frequently reduces the friction that develops when a sponsor does not trust what they are hearing from portfolio company management.

The fractional COO does not solve the board confidence problem by themselves. But they create the conditions for operational improvement that is the only real solution. And they do it faster than any alternative.

  • Board meetings have begun to focus primarily on operational problems rather than growth strategy
  • The sponsor's operating partners are increasing their involvement in day-to-day operational decisions
  • Board members have explicitly asked what the plan is to fix operational execution
  • A recent board or investor meeting ended with action items that belong to an operational executive the company does not currently have

What Happens If You Recognize More Than One of These Signs?

Most manufacturing companies that genuinely need a fractional COO recognize themselves in more than one of the five signs above. That is not coincidence — these patterns are related. A CEO spending 50% of their time on operational firefighting is likely the same company where OTD is declining and board confidence is eroding. The failure modes compound each other.

If you recognize two or more of these patterns clearly, the question is not whether you need operational leadership support. The question is how quickly you can put it in place, and what kind of experience profile it needs to have to address your specific situation.

The engagement structure matters as much as the individual. A well-designed fractional COO engagement for a manufacturing company includes a clear diagnostic phase, defined accountability metrics from the outset, weekly reporting cadences, and an explicit plan for building internal capability so that the company does not create permanent dependency on the fractional executive. If those elements are not present in the structure of an engagement you are evaluating, the model is unlikely to deliver the results you need.

The Cost of Waiting

Every quarter without the right operational leadership in a manufacturing company carries a real cost — in margin erosion, in customer attrition risk, in talent loss, in missed growth opportunities. A full-time COO search takes four to nine months and requires a candidate who will make $300,000 to $500,000 or more annually before factoring in benefits and equity. A fractional COO engagement can begin within two to four weeks of a decision and typically runs $10,000 to $18,000 per month for three days per week of senior operating leadership.

The economics are straightforward. The harder question is the organizational one: are you willing to recognize that the operational gap is real, that it is not going to close on its own, and that the cost of continuing without senior operational leadership is higher than the cost of addressing it directly?

If the answer is yes, the next step is a direct conversation about what you are dealing with and whether a fractional COO engagement is the right structure for it. That conversation does not require a commitment — it requires an honest description of where your operation is today and what it needs to get to where it needs to be.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should a manufacturing company hire a fractional COO?

A manufacturing company should hire a fractional COO when the CEO or founder is spending more than 40% of their time on operational firefighting, when on-time delivery is declining without clear ownership, when the business has crossed $15M–$20M in revenue and operational complexity has outpaced the team, during a post-merger integration that is stalling, or when the board or a PE sponsor is losing confidence in operational execution.

What are the signs you need a fractional COO?

The clearest signs you need a fractional COO include: the CEO is doing the COO's job instead of running the business; on-time delivery is declining and no single leader owns the outcome; revenue has scaled past $15M–$20M but operational systems have not kept pace; a post-acquisition integration is behind schedule or destroying acquired value; or investors and board members are expressing concern about operational performance.

How much does a fractional COO cost for a manufacturing company?

Fractional COO engagements for manufacturing companies typically range from $8,000 to $25,000 per month depending on time commitment, scope, and company size. This compares to $300,000 to $500,000 or more annually for a full-time COO including salary, benefits, and equity — without the four-to-nine month search delay.

Can a fractional COO help with PE-backed manufacturing companies?

Yes. Private equity sponsors frequently deploy fractional COOs in portfolio manufacturing companies to accelerate post-acquisition integration, restore on-time delivery performance, build the operational infrastructure required for a platform acquisition strategy, or stabilize a situation ahead of a growth capital raise. The fractional model also allows the sponsor to right-size operational leadership cost while the company scales to the point that justifies a full-time COO hire.

How is a fractional COO different from a manufacturing consultant?

A fractional COO holds operating authority — they manage people, own production decisions, and are accountable to measurable outcomes like OTD, yield, and EBITDA. A consultant delivers analysis and recommendations. The fractional COO is present at the daily production standup and drives accountability in real time; the consultant presents findings in a quarterly review and leaves implementation to the internal team.
